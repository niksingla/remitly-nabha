What the mid-market rate actually is, and why your bank never gives it to you

Most of what a transfer costs is not the fee. It is tucked into the exchange rate, where it is far harder to see — and that is not an accident.

NK Navjot Kaur 18 October 2026 6 min read
FX

A friend of mine sent money home every month for six years and never once looked at the exchange rate. She looked at the fee, because the fee was the number printed in bold. It was usually around two hundred rupees, which seemed reasonable for something that arrived the same day.

When we worked it out properly, the fee turned out to be about four percent of what the transfer was really costing her. The rest was in the rate. Over six years that gap added up to something close to a year of the support she was trying to send.

This is the most common and most expensive misunderstanding in cross-border payments, so it is worth taking apart slowly.

Currencies have two prices

When institutions trade currency with each other, there is never a single price. There is a price at which someone will buy and a slightly higher price at which someone will sell. The difference between those two is the spread, and it is how market makers get paid for standing ready to trade.

The midpoint between the buy price and the sell price is the mid-market rate. It is not a rate anyone actually transacts at. It is a reference point — the fairest single number you can quote for what a currency is worth at a given moment. When you search for an exchange rate online, or look at a financial news site, the mid-market rate is what you are shown.

The mid-market rate is the honest answer to "what is this currency worth?" Every rate a consumer is offered is that number, moved slightly in the provider's favour.

Where the margin hides

No provider gives you the mid-market rate, and it would be unreasonable to expect one to. Converting currency involves real costs: holding balances in both currencies, moving money between banking systems, absorbing the risk that the market shifts between your transfer and their settlement. That work has to be paid for.

The question is not whether there is a margin but how big it is and whether you can see it. Compare these two offers on the same transfer:

ProviderFeeRate offeredRecipient gets
Provider A₹00.01098$1,098.00
Provider B₹1990.01126$1,123.76

Both are converting ₹1,00,000. Provider A advertises a zero fee and is the more expensive of the two by roughly ₹2,300 once you account for the rate. The headline that reads as free is the one that costs more, which is precisely why it is the headline.

Working out the margin in thirty seconds

You need two numbers: the mid-market rate and the rate you are being offered. Then:

  1. Subtract the offered rate from the mid-market rate.
  2. Divide the result by the mid-market rate.
  3. Multiply by a hundred.

With a mid-market rate of 0.01131 and an offered rate of 0.01098, the difference is 0.00033. Divided by 0.01131 that is 0.0292, so the margin is 2.92 percent. On a ₹1,00,000 transfer, that single percentage is worth about ₹2,920 — more than fourteen times the fee that Provider B charges.

You can do this on a phone in the time it takes to read a quote. Our currency converter shows the reference rate, and most providers display the rate they are offering before you confirm.

The three questions worth asking

If you only remember one thing from this piece, make it this: compare the amount the recipient ends up with. It is the only figure that captures the fee, the rate, the payment surcharge and any deduction on arrival all at once.

Beyond that, there are three questions that cut through most marketing:

  • What rate are you applying, as a number? If the answer is "our rate" or "a competitive rate", a margin is being obscured.
  • Does the recipient's bank deduct anything on arrival? Some inbound credits carry a local charge the sender never sees.
  • Is the rate locked, and for how long? A quote that can drift between confirmation and settlement is not really a quote.

Why this stayed opaque for so long

Fees are easy to compare and rates are not. A customer can tell instantly that ₹199 is less than ₹450. Telling that 0.01098 is meaningfully worse than 0.01126 requires arithmetic, a reference rate and the knowledge that the comparison matters at all.

For decades that asymmetry was comfortable for the industry. Competing on the number people look at while quietly widening the number they do not is a reliable strategy, and it survives for as long as customers do not know where to look.

That is changing, slowly, mostly because enough providers started publishing their margin and made it awkward for the ones that did not. But the old pricing is still everywhere — at airport counters, on bank branch boards, and in apps that lead with the word "free".

The short version

The fee is the part you are shown. The rate is the part that matters. Find the mid-market rate, compare it against what you are offered, and judge every provider on the amount that lands in your family's account rather than the number printed in bold on the way in.
