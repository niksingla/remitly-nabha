Receiving money into India without registering

Inbound-only is genuinely cheaper, and without a company it can cost almost nothing. But the answer splits hard on one question: whose money are you receiving?

The short version Receiving your own money from abroad needs no licence, no company and no RBI approval — just a PAN and a bank account. Receiving money on behalf of other people needs an RBI authorisation that is only issued to registered companies. There is no unregistered version of the second one at any price.
The fork

Two very different questions

Everything below depends on which of these you are doing. They are not variations of the same business.

Allowed unregistered

You receive your own money

Freelance income, consulting fees, client payments, family support, rent from an overseas property. The money is yours, it lands in your own account, nobody else has a claim on it.

Cost: near zero to set up, then 0.5% – 4.5% per payment depending on the channel you pick.

Not allowed unregistered

You receive money for others

Collecting from senders abroad and paying out to recipients in India. Even taking a small cut, even for friends, even informally — this is a money transfer service and it needs authorisation.

Cost: ₹15 crore of net worth and a registered company to do it legally. Penalties up to three times the money moved if you do not.

Route A

Receiving your own money

No licence exists for this because none is needed. What you do need is paperwork, and most of it is free.

Whichever you use, it must be an authorised bank or an RBI-licensed cross-border payment aggregator. That is a real constraint, not a formality — it is what keeps you on Route A rather than Route B.

GST

0% on exports

Service exports are zero-rated when all five conditions in section 2(6) of the IGST Act are met. Below ₹20 lakh turnover you need not register at all.

TCS

None

TCS applies to money leaving India under the LRS. Inbound remittances attract none of it.

Income tax

Slab rates

On profit, not on receipts. Presumptive schemes can apply — 50% deemed profit under 44ADA up to ₹75 lakh for specified professions, or 6% under 44AD up to ₹3 crore if receipts are digital.

Money must arrive within nine months of the invoice date under the FEMA export rules in force from 1 October 2026 — twelve if settled in rupees.

Route B

Receiving money for other people

This is what the rest of this site pretends to do. Here is why it cannot be done unregistered.

PA-CB inward transactions are capped at ₹25 lakh per unit of goods or services, inward and outward funds cannot be co-mingled or netted off, and the inward collection account cannot be pre-funded.

Doing it anyway is a FEMA contravention, not a paperwork gap Dealing in foreign exchange with anyone who is not an authorised person breaches section 3(a) of FEMA. That is the one category of contravention the RBI cannot compound, so there is no administrative settlement available — it goes to adjudication.

On ₹1 crore of transfers routed through an unauthorised channel, the penalty ceiling alone is ₹3 crore — before confiscation of the ₹1 crore itself.

So the honest summary is that the cheap version of this business exists, but it is not a business — it is one person invoicing foreign clients and keeping their own money. The moment a second person's money passes through your account, the floor jumps from zero to ₹15 crore. See the full cost and licensing breakdown for what that involves.

Where these figures come from

  • FEMA 1999 sections 3, 13, 14 and 15, and the Foreign Exchange (Compounding Proceedings) Rules 2024
  • RBI Master Direction on Payment Aggregators, including the PA-CB provisions and escrow account table
  • RBI Master Direction on the Money Transfer Service Scheme
  • IGST Act section 2(6) and Notification 10/2017-Integrated Tax for the ₹20 lakh threshold
  • Channel pricing is from published provider schedules and moves often — check before relying on it
