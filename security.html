Keeping money and accounts safe

Platform security

What sits behind an account

The controls described here are the ones a licensed provider would be expected to operate.

Encryption throughout

Data encrypted in transit with current TLS and at rest in storage, with keys rotated on a schedule and held separately from the data.

Two-factor authentication

A second factor on login and on any sensitive change, with step-up verification when a device or location looks unfamiliar.

Continuous monitoring

Every transfer scored against patterns associated with fraud and money laundering, with unusual activity held for review rather than auto-approved.

Safeguarded funds

Customer money held in segregated accounts at regulated banks, kept apart from the company's own operating funds.

Independent audit

Annual penetration testing by an external firm, plus regular internal review of access rights and privileged accounts.

Least-privilege access

Staff see only what their role requires, every access to customer data is logged, and nobody can move money alone.

Important Remitly Nabha is a fictional brand and a static demonstration site. It holds no customer funds, is not licensed anywhere and operates none of the controls described above. This page exists to show how a security page is written.
Fraud awareness

Scams that target people sending money

The schemes below are the ones that most reliably separate people from their savings. They are worth recognising even if you never use a service like this.

Very common

The romance scam

Someone you have only met online builds a relationship over weeks or months, then hits a crisis — a medical bill, a customs charge, a stranded flight. The request is always urgent and there is always a reason you cannot meet or video call.

The tell: a sustained emotional relationship with someone you have never met in person, followed by a request for money.

Very common

The advance fee

You have won a prize, been approved for a loan, or inherited from a relative you did not know. To release it, you must first pay a processing fee, a tax or a legal charge. Pay it and a second fee appears.

The tell: money you are owed cannot be released until you send money. Legitimate payouts never work this way.

Rising

The fake rental

A flat listed well below market rate, a landlord conveniently abroad, and a deposit required today to hold it against other interested tenants. The property is usually real and lifted from another listing.

The tell: a deposit requested before any viewing, with pressure created by invented competition.

Rising

The invoice redirect

A supplier emails to say their bank details have changed, just before a payment is due. The email looks right because the account it came from has been compromised, or the domain is off by one character.

The tell: changed payment details arriving by email. Always confirm on a phone number you already had.

Persistent

The impersonated official

A caller claims to be from a tax office, a bank's fraud team, or a police unit. There is a problem with your account and funds must be moved immediately to a "safe" account to protect them.

The tell: no genuine authority will ever ask you to transfer money to secure it, or ask for a one-time code.

Persistent

The family emergency

A message from a relative's hacked account, or from an unfamiliar number claiming to be them with a new phone. They are in trouble, cannot talk, and need money now.

The tell: urgency combined with an inability to speak. Hang up and call the number you already have for them.

One question

The question that breaks most scams

Before sending money to anyone, ask yourself: would I still be comfortable with this if I waited twenty-four hours?

Almost every scam depends on urgency, because urgency prevents you from checking. A genuine landlord will let you view a flat. A genuine supplier will take a phone call. A real relative will be reachable on the number you already have. Nobody with a legitimate claim on your money loses anything if you take a day to verify it.

If a delay would supposedly cause disaster — the flat goes to someone else, the prize expires, the account is seized tonight — that pressure is the product being sold to you.

We will never ask you to

  • Share a one-time passcode with anyone, including support staff
  • Tell us your full password over chat, email or a phone call
  • Install remote access software so somebody can "help" you
  • Move your money to a different account to keep it safe
  • Pay a fee to release a transfer that is already on its way

Any contact asking for one of these is fraudulent, regardless of how convincing the caller ID, email address or website looks.

If it has already happened

What to do after sending money to a scam

  1. Act in the first hour

    Contact the provider immediately. If the transfer has not been collected or deposited, it can usually be stopped outright.

  2. Tell your bank

    If you paid by card or bank transfer, your bank may be able to attempt a recall and will flag the receiving details.

  3. Report it

    File a report with your local police or national fraud reporting service. Reports are what get receiving accounts closed.

  4. Keep everything

    Save messages, profiles, phone numbers and account details. Do not delete the conversation, however uncomfortable it is.

  5. Secure your accounts

    Change passwords, enable two-factor authentication and sign out of all devices in case details were harvested too.

  6. Expect a second approach

    Victims are frequently contacted again by someone offering to recover the money for a fee. That is the same scam, round two.
