Money should move as easily as people do

Around 280 million people live outside the country they were born in, and a great many of them send money back. That flow is one of the largest and least glamorous parts of the global economy — and it has been expensive and opaque for far too long.

Our purpose

The problem we set out to describe

Sending money across a border used to mean a branch visit, a paper form, a rate you were told rather than shown, and a fee that bore no relationship to the work involved. The person on the receiving end often lost a further slice on collection.

Remitly Nabha is a design exercise in doing the opposite: show the rate, show the fee, show the exact amount that arrives, and then get out of the way. Every page on this site exists to demonstrate how that promise would be presented to someone deciding whether to trust it with their salary.

It is a portfolio build rather than a business. But the problem it describes is real, and the interface patterns here are the ones a genuine product would need to get right.

How we work

Four principles behind the design

Say the number

Anywhere a cost exists, it is written down. No "rates may vary" in place of an actual figure.

Design for the recipient

The person collecting the money is as much a user as the person sending it.

Be boring about trust

Security and compliance copy is plain and specific, not decorated with padlock imagery.

Work on a slow phone

Semantic HTML, no framework overhead, and layouts that hold up at 320 pixels wide.

Timeline

How the project came together

A fictional history, written to show how an about page handles a company narrative.

  1. Stage one

    A spreadsheet and a complaint

    It started with working out how much a family was actually losing per year on monthly transfers. The answer was most of a month's rent.

  2. Stage two

    The pricing model

    Two speed tiers, a visible margin and a flat fee that disappears above a threshold. Everything in the interface follows from that decision.

  3. Stage three

    Payout methods

    Bank deposit alone was not enough. Wallets, cash counters and home delivery each got their own flow and their own set of requirements.

  4. Stage four

    The business side

    Batch uploads, approvals and an API, because the same payout rails serve a studio paying six contractors as well as a family.

  5. Stage five

    Everything around the edges

    Help centre, country directory, converter, security guidance and the legal pages — the parts that make a payments site feel real.
